# 3 Thinking Habits That Make You Look Smarter

Source: Justin Sung, newsletter

- Many smart people get no recognition for the quality of their thinking.
- Some people sound smart but, on reflection, say nothing valuable.
- Three thinking habits help people sound smart and get deserved recognition. Making them genuine
  habits also makes people smarter over time.

## You don't deserve more recognition

- People judge others by how they present their thoughts, not by the thoughts themselves.
- When a listener struggles to follow a good explanation, they don't blame themselves. They conclude
  the speaker's thinking is poor and disorganised.
- Most high performers have a "legibility" of thinking problem (others find their thinking hard to
  follow), not a "quality" of thinking problem.
- The habits below improve legibility and also genuinely improve thinking.

## Habit 1: Start at the end

- Example: the author has worked with world-leading researchers who are painfully confusing to
  listen to. Asked for an opinion, they walk through an elaborate chain of reasoning, and listeners
  lose the thread 10 minutes before the conclusion arrives.
- Practice: state the conclusion first, then the reasoning. See [[bluf-writing-style]].
- Reason: this frames the issue, so the listener knows how to interpret what follows.
- This works even when unsure of the conclusion: state current thinking and where uncertain.

## Habit 2: Separate your observations from your conclusions

- The author's pet peeve: being told about a problem and given solutions straight away. The author
  likes solution-focused thinking but dislikes having to wonder how the person identified the
  problem and the right solutions.
- "Smart" people know a set of observations can be interpreted in many ways.
- Practice: state observations first, then the conclusions drawn from them.
- Benefits:
  1. Shows the listener the reasoning.
  2. Builds trust in the conclusions.
  3. Lets everyone discuss their own interpretations of the observations, which is much better than
     arguing over opinions.

## Habit 3: Clarify the question

- Practice: when asked for input, restate the question. Examples:
  - "So what you're really asking is if this is worth the cost?"
  - "I think there are really two questions here which we should address separately."
  - "It sounds like the real concern is xyz, is that right?"
- Being on the receiving end feels good: it shows the person is thinking deeply and trying to
  understand the core issue, and it helps the asker organise their own thoughts.

## This week…

- Try one or all of the habits the next time thoughts need communicating.
- If thoughts become disorganised while trying, the bottleneck is how the person thinks, not their
  communication.
- In that case, improve thinking frameworks, e.g. through training programmes such as the author's.
